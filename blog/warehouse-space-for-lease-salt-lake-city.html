How to Find Warehouse Space in Salt Lake City: A Guide for Small Businesses

Looking for warehouse or industrial space in Salt Lake City? Here’s what to know about location, costs, building requirements, and finding the right property for your business.

Abby Hammer
Hammer Realty & Co. · Logical Move Brokerage

Finding warehouse space in Salt Lake City can be more complicated than it initially appears.

You might start by searching for warehouse space for lease in Salt Lake City, browsing commercial listings, and scheduling a few property tours.

But finding the right warehouse involves much more than choosing a building with the right square footage and monthly rent.

For small businesses, particularly those looking for 2,000–15,000 square feet of industrial or flex space, the wrong property can create expensive operational challenges.

Whether you're expanding a manufacturing operation, relocating a distribution business, or looking for your first warehouse, here's how to approach your search.

1. Determine how much warehouse space you actually need

Before searching available properties, start by understanding your business's space requirements.

Square footage is important, but how that space is configured matters just as much.

For example, two warehouses might each offer 5,000 square feet, but one may have significantly more usable storage space because of its ceiling height, column spacing, or office layout.

Consider the following:

  • Storage requirements: How much inventory, equipment, or material do you need to accommodate?
  • Office space: Do you need private offices, a reception area, conference rooms, or just a small administrative workspace?
  • Clear height: How much vertical storage space do you need? Taller ceilings may allow more efficient racking and storage.
  • Loading access: Do you need dock-high loading, grade-level doors, or both?
  • Parking: How many employee, customer, and commercial vehicle parking spaces will you need?
  • Future growth: Will the property still accommodate your business three to five years from now?

A 3,000-square-foot flex unit might be perfect for a growing service company with a small office and equipment storage.

A distribution business with significant inventory and frequent deliveries may need a very different layout, even at the same square footage.

Tip: Before touring properties, create a list of your must-have features and your nice-to-have features. This will help you narrow your search and avoid wasting time on properties that won't work for your operations.

2. Choose the right Salt Lake City location

Location affects much more than your business address.

It can influence transportation costs, employee commutes, customer access, delivery efficiency, and your ability to grow.

The Salt Lake Valley offers several areas worth evaluating for industrial and warehouse space.

Salt Lake City

Industrial areas in and around Salt Lake City may appeal to businesses that need access to major transportation corridors, customers, suppliers, and the Salt Lake City International Airport.

Availability, pricing, and building configurations vary by submarket.

West Valley City

West Valley City is worth considering for businesses seeking industrial, warehouse, or flex space with access to the broader Salt Lake Valley.

Depending on the property, businesses may find options suitable for contractors, distributors, light manufacturing, and storage operations.

South Salt Lake

South Salt Lake can be attractive to businesses that want a relatively central location and convenient access to customers throughout the valley.

Some industrial properties offer smaller configurations that may work well for local service businesses and light industrial users.

Murray and Midvale

Businesses that serve customers across the Salt Lake Valley may want to evaluate industrial and flex opportunities in Murray or Midvale.

Inventory and permitted uses vary, so it's important to evaluate individual properties rather than assuming every industrial building will accommodate your operation.

Draper and Sandy

Businesses serving the southern Salt Lake Valley may prefer locations in Draper or Sandy, particularly when employee access, customer proximity, or a combination of office and warehouse space is important.

Other areas worth considering

Depending on your business needs, you may also want to explore properties in North Salt Lake, Woods Cross, West Jordan, South Jordan, or nearby communities.

The right location ultimately depends on where your employees, customers, suppliers, and deliveries need to be.

Don't limit your search to one city simply because it's where your current business is located. Expanding your search area can uncover properties that better fit your operational requirements.

3. Understand the different types of industrial space

Not every property advertised as warehouse space serves the same purpose.

Understanding the differences can save considerable time during your search.

Traditional warehouse space

Warehouse properties are generally designed for storage, distribution, and related operations.

Common features may include:

  • Open storage areas
  • Dock-high or grade-level loading
  • Higher clear heights
  • Truck access
  • Limited office space

These properties may be appropriate for distributors, inventory-heavy businesses, and companies requiring substantial storage capacity.

Flex space

Flex space typically combines warehouse or light industrial areas with office, showroom, or other business functions.

For example, a 4,000-square-foot flex unit might include 1,000 square feet of office space and 3,000 square feet of warehouse space.

Flex properties can be particularly useful for contractors, small manufacturers, service companies, and businesses that need both administrative and operational space. Learn more about how we help businesses find industrial and flex space in Salt Lake City.

Manufacturing space

Manufacturing operations often require specialized infrastructure beyond what a typical warehouse provides.

Depending on the business, important considerations may include:

  • Electrical capacity and voltage
  • Three-phase power availability
  • Floor load capacity
  • Ventilation and exhaust systems
  • Equipment access
  • Water and drainage requirements
  • Zoning and permitted uses

A building that works well for storage may not be suitable for manufacturing.

Before signing a lease, verify that the property's infrastructure and legally permitted uses support your operations.

4. Know what warehouse space really costs

One of the most common mistakes businesses make when comparing warehouse listings is focusing only on the advertised rental rate.

Commercial industrial leases may use different rent structures, including triple-net (NNN) and gross or modified-gross arrangements.

Understanding NNN leases

With a triple-net lease, tenants typically pay base rent plus additional expenses associated with the property, such as:

  • Property taxes
  • Building insurance
  • Common area maintenance or other operating expenses

The exact charges and responsibilities depend on the lease.

Hypothetical example: estimating rent on a 5,000-square-foot warehouse

Imagine a 5,000-square-foot warehouse advertised at an annual base rental rate of $15 per square foot, with estimated NNN expenses of $4 per square foot annually.

Hypothetical rent calculation for a 5,000-square-foot warehouse
Cost Calculation Annual Monthly
Base rent 5,000 SF × $15 $75,000 $6,250
Estimated NNN expenses 5,000 SF × $4 $20,000 ≈ $1,667
Estimated occupancy cost Base rent + NNN $95,000 ≈ $7,917

In this example, your estimated monthly occupancy cost would be approximately $7,917 before separately billed utilities, certain maintenance obligations, and other potential costs.

Important: These figures are hypothetical examples to illustrate how commercial rent is calculated. They are not current Salt Lake City market rental rates.

When comparing properties, request a breakdown of all anticipated occupancy expenses rather than relying on the advertised base rent alone.

Also ask whether operating expenses are estimates, how they are reconciled, and what increases you could be responsible for during the lease.

5. Check the building's operational requirements

A warehouse might look perfect online but be completely wrong for your business once you evaluate its infrastructure.

Before committing to a property, investigate the following.

Loading doors and truck access

Does the building have dock-high loading, grade-level loading, or both? Can delivery trucks maneuver safely? Is there adequate room for loading and unloading?

For businesses receiving frequent deliveries, poor truck access can become a daily operational problem.

Electrical capacity

This is especially important for manufacturing businesses.

Verify whether the property has the electrical service your equipment requires, including appropriate voltage, amperage, and phase.

Don't assume that electrical upgrades will be inexpensive or feasible.

Clear height and storage configuration

The usable height of a warehouse affects how much inventory you can store vertically.

Also consider column spacing, fire protection requirements, and whether your planned racking configuration is permitted.

Heating, cooling, and ventilation

Not all warehouses are fully heated or air-conditioned.

Consider employee working conditions, product storage requirements, and any specialized ventilation needs.

Zoning and permitted uses

Before signing a lease, confirm that your intended business activities are permitted at the property.

A property marketed as industrial space may still have restrictions affecting manufacturing, automotive work, outdoor storage, customer-facing uses, or other operations.

Verify requirements with the applicable municipality and relevant authorities rather than relying solely on a listing description.

6. Search beyond the major commercial listing websites

Many business owners begin their warehouse search on commercial real estate websites such as Crexi or LoopNet.

These platforms can be useful for understanding available properties and comparing advertised asking rents.

However, online listings don't always tell the whole story.

Some properties may be marketed through brokerage networks, while others may have outdated availability information or incomplete details.

A commercial tenant representative can help you research suitable options, contact listing brokers, confirm availability, and coordinate property tours.

Instead of spending hours contacting multiple listing agents, you can work with one representative who understands your business requirements and helps organize the search.

It's also worth remembering that a listing agent generally represents the landlord's interests, not yours.

7. Compare properties based on total business impact

Once you've identified several potential properties, resist the temptation to choose the one with the lowest advertised rent.

Instead, compare how each location will affect your business.

Consider:

  • Total monthly occupancy costs
  • Transportation and delivery expenses
  • Employee accessibility
  • Warehouse layout and operational efficiency
  • Required improvements
  • Moving and equipment installation costs
  • Future expansion opportunities
  • Lease terms and flexibility

For example, a warehouse that costs $500 more per month might still be the better option if it significantly reduces delivery times or eliminates the need for off-site storage.

Similarly, a less expensive property could become costly if it requires major electrical upgrades or substantial tenant improvements.

8. Negotiate before you sign

Once you've found a suitable property, the advertised lease terms are not necessarily the final terms.

Depending on the property, landlord, and market conditions, you may be able to negotiate:

  • Base rental rate
  • Annual rent increases
  • Free or reduced rent periods
  • Tenant improvement allowances
  • Lease duration and renewal options
  • Maintenance and repair responsibilities
  • Expansion options
  • Timing of possession and lease commencement

For businesses moving into industrial space, it's especially important to address who is responsible for necessary improvements and when the property will be ready for occupancy. Have qualified legal counsel review your lease before signing, particularly if it contains substantial financial obligations or specialized operational requirements.
