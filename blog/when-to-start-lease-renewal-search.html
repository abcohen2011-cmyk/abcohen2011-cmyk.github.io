When Should You Start Your Commercial Lease Renewal Search?

Most Utah businesses wait too long. By the time the landlord brings up renewal, the window to negotiate — or walk — has already closed. Here's how to time it right.

Abby Hammer
Hammer Realty & Co. · Logical Move Brokerage

If your commercial lease expires in the next two years and you haven't started thinking about it yet, you're not alone — and you still have time. But if it's expiring in six months and renewal hasn't come up, you're in a weaker position than you realize.

Lease renewal timing is the single most underestimated factor in commercial real estate. Most business owners focus on the terms — the rent, the length, the build-out allowance — when the real lever is much simpler: when you start.

Why Timing Is Everything

Your landlord's broker is a professional. Their job is to fill the space at the best possible rate, and they know exactly how long it takes to find an alternative tenant for your type of space. They also know when your lease expires — often better than you do.

Once you're inside the window where relocating becomes impractical — usually six to nine months before expiration — your negotiating position changes. At that point, the cost and disruption of moving is real and visible. The landlord knows it. And the conversation about renewal terms shifts accordingly.

The earlier you start, the more genuine your alternatives are. And real alternatives are the only thing that creates real leverage.

The Renewal Timeline: When to Do What

18–24 monthsbefore expiration
The ideal starting point
Begin surveying the market. What's available? What are comparable businesses paying? This isn't a commitment to move — it's information gathering that shapes everything that follows.
12–18 monthsbefore expiration
Open conversations with your landlord
This is when to raise renewal with your landlord — not to negotiate, but to signal you're paying attention. Their response tells you a lot about how motivated they are to keep you.
9–12 monthsbefore expiration
Negotiate in earnest
Now you negotiate renewal terms — or seriously evaluate alternatives. You still have enough time to relocate if the terms don't work. That optionality is what keeps your landlord honest.
6 monthsbefore expiration
Decision point — still workable, but tighter
You can still negotiate, but a move at this point is stressful and expensive. Your landlord knows it. You may end up accepting terms you would have pushed back on earlier.
Under 6 monthsbefore expiration
Survival mode
Relocation is now very difficult. The landlord can drive harder terms, and you have little room to push back without risking your operations. Avoid this if at all possible.

What "Starting Early" Actually Looks Like

Starting the process 18–24 months out doesn't mean signing a lease or committing to anything. It means getting oriented:

  • Pulling comparable listings to understand what similar space is leasing for right now
  • Revisiting your space needs — will your business be larger, smaller, or different in two years?
  • Checking your current lease for any renewal notice requirements (many leases require 6–12 months' written notice to exercise a renewal option, or you lose it)
  • Identifying two or three alternative locations worth tracking, even if you have no intention of moving

That last point is the most important. You don't need to want to move. You just need credible alternatives. Without them, your landlord's opening offer is also their final offer.

Check your lease for notice requirements before anything else. Many commercial leases include a renewal option — the right to extend at a predetermined rate or at market — but they require written notice 6, 9, or even 12 months in advance. Miss that window, and the option expires. Your landlord isn't required to remind you.

Common Mistakes Utah Businesses Make

Waiting for the landlord to bring it up

Landlords are not in a hurry to start negotiations on your timeline. They'll raise renewal when it's convenient for them — often later than you'd want. Waiting for them means starting the conversation closer to your expiration than is ideal.

Assuming renewal is automatic

Even if you have a good relationship with your landlord, renewal terms are not guaranteed to be favorable. Market conditions, the landlord's other tenants, and local vacancy rates all affect what they'll offer. Don't assume the past predicts the renewal.

Focusing only on rent

Rent is the most visible number, but it's not the only one. Annual escalations (3% compounding over five years is a significant number), tenant improvement allowances, operating expense caps, and exit rights all affect the real cost of a lease. Negotiating only on base rent and accepting everything else is a common and costly mistake.

Not knowing what the market looks like

If you don't know what comparable space is leasing for, you can't evaluate whether your landlord's offer is reasonable. A tenant rep can pull comps quickly — but even doing a basic search yourself gives you a foundation for the conversation.

What a Tenant Representative Does in This Process

A tenant representative works exclusively for you — not the landlord, and not both sides. In a lease renewal, that means:

  • Pulling market comparables so you know what similar space is actually trading for
  • Reviewing your existing lease for renewal options, notice requirements, and any clauses that affect negotiation
  • Running a parallel search for alternative space — giving you real options, not hypothetical ones
  • Negotiating directly with the landlord on rent, escalations, TI allowances, and lease structure

The Short Version

If your lease expires in 18–24 months, now is the right time to start. If it expires in 12 months, you're behind but not out. If it expires in six months and you haven't started, get moving today — and be realistic that your options are narrower.

The best lease renewals aren't won at the negotiating table. They're won in the months before the negotiation starts, when you're building alternatives and gathering information. That's the work that determines your leverage — and leverage determines your outcome.
