Renew or Relocate? How to Decide What’s Best for Your Business

An essential guide for Salt Lake City and Northern Utah businesses approaching a commercial lease renewal.

Abby Hammer
Hammer Realty & Co. · Logical Move Brokerage

Your commercial lease is coming up for renewal. Your landlord has offered you another three or five years, and staying in your current space seems like the easiest option.

But is it the best option for your business?

For many small business owners, renewing a commercial lease feels like the obvious choice. Moving requires time, money, and coordination. If the current location is working reasonably well, why disrupt things?

The answer is simple: Your business may have changed significantly since you signed your last lease.

Whether you operate a warehouse, manufacturing facility, retail storefront, or office, a lease renewal is an opportunity to evaluate whether your current space still supports your operations, growth plans, and financial goals.

Here's how to determine whether renewing or relocating makes the most sense.

1. Start with your business, not the building

Before comparing rental rates or browsing available properties, evaluate what your business actually needs.

Think about how your operations have changed over the past few years.

  • Has your team grown or become smaller?
  • Are you running out of warehouse or inventory storage?
  • Does your current layout create inefficiencies?
  • Would a different location improve customer access?
  • Are you paying for square footage you no longer use?
  • Do you anticipate significant growth over the next three to five years?

For example, a distribution business leasing 4,000 square feet of industrial space in Salt Lake City may have doubled its inventory since signing its original lease.

The business might technically still fit in the building, but crowded storage areas, inefficient loading, and limited room for expansion could be creating unnecessary operational costs.

In that situation, relocating to a larger, more functional warehouse might make financial sense, even if the monthly rent increases.

The goal isn't necessarily to find cheaper space. It's to find space that supports your business.

2. Compare your current rent to the market

One of the most important steps before renewing a commercial lease is understanding how your current rental rate compares to similar properties.

Just because your landlord offers a renewal doesn't mean the proposed terms are competitive.

Commercial rental rates can change considerably over a three-, five-, or ten-year lease.

Before accepting a renewal proposal, research comparable properties based on:

  • Location and accessibility
  • Square footage and building condition
  • Property type and functionality
  • Base rental rate
  • Operating expenses and additional charges
  • Lease term and annual increases
  • Available concessions and tenant improvements

For example, if you're leasing industrial space in Murray, it may be worth comparing available properties in South Salt Lake, West Valley City, or other nearby areas.

You might discover that your current rate is competitive.

Or you might find that other landlords are offering more favorable terms, newer facilities, or space that better accommodates your operations.

Either way, having accurate market information helps you make a more informed decision.

3. Calculate the true cost of relocating

Moving your business involves more than paying a moving company.

Depending on your operations, relocation expenses may include:

  • Professional moving and equipment transportation
  • Tenant improvements and construction
  • New signage and branding
  • Utility and internet installation
  • Permits and inspections
  • Employee travel or commute changes
  • Temporary operational downtime
  • Overlapping rent payments
  • Restoration obligations at your existing location

For industrial and manufacturing businesses, moving specialized equipment can be particularly expensive.

A relocation that saves $1,000 per month in rent might look attractive initially. But if the move costs $60,000, it could take years to recover that expense through rent savings alone.

Example: comparing a renewal to a relocation

Imagine a small business currently occupying 5,000 square feet of commercial space.

Expense Renew current lease Relocate
Monthly occupancy cost $6,000 $5,000
One-time relocation expenses $0 $45,000
Five-year occupancy cost $360,000 $300,000
Total five-year cost $360,000 $345,000

In this simplified example, relocating saves approximately $15,000 over five years.

However, this comparison doesn't account for annual rent increases, financing costs, the time value of money, or differences in operational efficiency.

And if the new space improves productivity, reduces transportation costs, or supports business growth, the financial benefits could be greater.

Conversely, if the move creates downtime or unexpected expenses, renewing might be the better option.

The important takeaway: Compare the total financial impact, not just the advertised rent.

4. Consider the hidden costs of staying

Relocating has obvious expenses. But staying in the wrong space can also cost your business money.

These costs are often harder to identify because they become part of your everyday operations. For example:

Inefficient warehouse layouts

Poor loading access, inadequate storage configurations, or insufficient ceiling heights can reduce productivity.

Limited room for growth

If your business is approaching capacity, staying could eventually require off-site storage or a second location.

Employee challenges

A location that creates long commutes or difficult parking may affect hiring and retention.

Customer accessibility

Retail and service businesses may benefit from improved visibility, parking, or proximity to their customers.

Building maintenance

Older properties may create recurring repair issues, depending on the responsibilities established in your lease.

These factors deserve consideration alongside your monthly rent.

A slightly more expensive building that operates more efficiently may be a better long-term investment for your business.

5. Remember that your current lease is negotiable

Many business owners assume that renewing their lease means accepting the landlord's proposed rental rate and signing another agreement.

But lease renewals can involve negotiations, just like new leases.

Depending on your lease, the property, and market conditions, potential negotiation points may include:

  • Base rental rate
  • Annual rent increases
  • Lease length
  • Tenant improvement allowances
  • Free or reduced rent periods
  • Maintenance responsibilities
  • Renewal and expansion options

Your existing lease may also contain specific renewal provisions, deadlines, or predetermined terms that affect your options. Review these provisions carefully before negotiating.

And remember that your landlord may have an incentive to retain a reliable tenant.

Vacancies can create expenses for property owners, including lost rental income, marketing costs, brokerage commissions, and potential improvements required for a new tenant.

That doesn't guarantee your landlord will offer better terms, but it may create an opportunity for a productive negotiation.

6. Understand the timing of your decision

One of the biggest mistakes businesses make is waiting until their lease is almost expired to evaluate alternatives.

If your lease expires in 60 days, you may have limited time to locate another property, negotiate a new lease, complete improvements, and relocate.

That can leave you with fewer practical options.

A general planning timeline looks like this:

  • 12 months before expiration: Review your lease, evaluate business needs, and begin researching the market.
  • 9 months before expiration: Compare renewal and relocation options, tour suitable properties, and estimate potential moving costs.
  • 6 months before expiration: Work toward a decision and begin negotiating lease terms.
  • 3 months before expiration: Ideally, finalize agreements and coordinate any necessary improvements or relocation plans.

These are planning milestones, not universal deadlines. Businesses with specialized equipment, significant construction needs, or limited available inventory may need substantially more time. For a stage-by-stage checklist, see Your Commercial Lease Expires in 12 Months: Here's Your Action Plan.

And most importantly, your existing lease's notice requirements take priority over any general timeline.

7. When does renewing make the most sense?

Renewing your current lease may be the better option when:

  • Your existing space meets your operational needs
  • Your rental rate is competitive with the market
  • Relocation expenses outweigh the potential benefits
  • Your current location is valuable to employees or customers
  • Your landlord is willing to negotiate reasonable terms
  • The property can accommodate your expected growth

If the building is working well and the financial terms make sense, there may be no reason to move.

The key is making that decision after evaluating your options, rather than simply choosing the path of least resistance.

8. When should you seriously consider relocating?

Relocating may be worth exploring when:

  • Your business has outgrown its current space
  • Your rent is significantly above comparable properties
  • Your building limits operational efficiency
  • Your location no longer serves your customers or employees
  • Your landlord is unwilling to negotiate reasonable terms
  • You need specialized facilities or additional infrastructure
  • A different property could better support your growth plans

Even if you ultimately stay, researching alternative properties can help you understand what's available and what your current space is worth.

The bottom line: make an informed decision before you renew

Renewing your commercial lease isn't necessarily the wrong decision.

And relocating isn't automatically the better one.

The right choice depends on your business's operational needs, financial goals, growth plans, and available alternatives.

What matters most is taking the time to evaluate your options before committing to another multi-year lease.
