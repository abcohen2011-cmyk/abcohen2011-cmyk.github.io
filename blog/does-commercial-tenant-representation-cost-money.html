Does It Cost Money to Use a Commercial Real Estate Agent as a Tenant?

Understanding tenant representation fees and why small businesses should consider having their own commercial real estate advisor.

Abby Hammer
Hammer Realty & Co. · Logical Move Brokerage

If you're a small business owner looking for warehouse, industrial, office, or retail space, you might assume that hiring a commercial real estate agent means adding another expense to your budget.

But here's something many business owners don't realize:

In many commercial leasing transactions, you can have an agent representing your interests without paying an additional out-of-pocket brokerage fee.

So how does that work, and why would you want your own representation when leasing commercial space?

Let's break it down.

Who pays the commercial real estate agent?

In many commercial leasing transactions, the landlord agrees to pay a brokerage commission as part of the leasing arrangement.

Depending on the transaction, that commission may be shared between the landlord's listing brokerage and the brokerage representing the tenant.

For example, imagine you're looking for 5,000 square feet of industrial space in Salt Lake City.

You could contact the landlord's listing agent directly, tour the property, and negotiate lease terms.

Or you could work with a tenant representative who helps you evaluate properties, compare options, and negotiate on your behalf.

In transactions where the landlord has agreed to compensate the tenant's brokerage, you may receive that representation without an additional out-of-pocket brokerage fee.

However, commission arrangements aren't identical in every transaction. Some properties may not offer cooperating brokerage compensation, and your representation agreement may establish other payment obligations.

Ask first. Before beginning a property search, ask your agent to explain how their brokerage will be compensated and whether you could be responsible for any fees.

What is a tenant representative?

A tenant representative is a commercial real estate professional who works on behalf of the business leasing the property.

While a landlord's listing agent represents the property owner's interests, a tenant representative focuses on the needs and objectives of the business looking for space.

That distinction matters.

A commercial lease can create financial obligations lasting several years. The decisions you make during the leasing process can affect your operating expenses, flexibility, and ability to grow.

A tenant representative can help you:

  • Identify properties that fit your operational needs and budget
  • Compare available spaces and their total occupancy costs
  • Evaluate renewal options at your current location
  • Negotiate rental rates, lease terms, and potential concessions
  • Coordinate property tours and communication with landlords
  • Navigate the process from initial search through lease execution

Your agent can also help you recognize when a property that looks attractive on paper may not be the right fit for your business.

Why not just call the landlord's agent directly?

You certainly can contact a listing agent directly. In fact, many small business owners begin their search that way.

But it's important to understand whom that agent represents.

A landlord's listing agent is generally hired to market the property and negotiate on the owner's behalf. Their responsibility is to their client: the landlord.

That doesn't mean the listing agent can't provide useful information or conduct a professional transaction. It simply means their representation is different from having someone specifically advocating for your business.

For example, suppose you're considering a warehouse listed at $15 per square foot annually.

The advertised rate might be only one component of your total occupancy cost.

You may also need to evaluate:

  • Common area maintenance charges
  • Property taxes and insurance
  • Annual rent increases
  • Maintenance and repair obligations
  • Tenant improvement costs
  • Renewal options and future flexibility

A tenant representative can help you evaluate these factors together rather than focusing only on the advertised rental rate.

Can a tenant representative help with a lease renewal?

Absolutely.

You don't have to be relocating to benefit from tenant representation.

If your existing commercial lease is approaching expiration, a tenant representative can help you evaluate whether renewing or relocating makes the most sense.

That might include reviewing your current lease terms, researching comparable properties, evaluating alternative locations, and negotiating renewal terms with your landlord.

Even if you love your current location, understanding what else is available can help you make a more informed decision.

One important consideration: brokerage compensation for lease renewals may differ from compensation for new leases. Before engaging an agent, clarify how renewal representation will be compensated.

If your lease expires within the next 12–18 months, it's worth beginning that conversation early, especially if relocating would involve construction, equipment, or operational changes. For a stage-by-stage timeline, see Your Commercial Lease Expires in 12 Months: Here's Your Action Plan.

What can you negotiate in a commercial lease?

Many business owners focus primarily on the monthly rental rate.

But commercial leases involve much more than rent.

Depending on the property, landlord, and market conditions, you may be able to negotiate:

Rental rate and annual increases

The starting rent matters, but so does how much it increases throughout the lease term.

Tenant improvement allowances

If the space requires modifications, the landlord may be willing to contribute toward certain improvements.

Free or reduced rent

Some landlords offer rent concessions to help offset moving costs, improvements, or the time needed to establish operations.

Lease term and renewal options

The right lease length can provide stability while preserving flexibility for future growth.

Maintenance and repair responsibilities

Understanding who pays for HVAC repairs, roof maintenance, and other building expenses can help prevent costly surprises.

Expansion or relocation flexibility

For growing businesses, provisions addressing future space needs may be especially valuable.

Not every term is negotiable in every transaction. But understanding your options before signing can make a meaningful difference.

What about industrial and warehouse tenants?

For industrial and flex-space tenants, the right building involves much more than square footage and price.

Imagine two available warehouses in the Salt Lake Valley. Both offer 5,000 square feet at similar advertised rental rates.

But one has sufficient electrical capacity, convenient loading access, and a layout that works for your equipment. The other requires expensive modifications and creates operational challenges.

The lower-cost option on paper may not actually be the better financial decision.

A commercial tenant representative can help you compare properties based on how your business operates, not just how the listing looks online.

The bottom line

If you're considering leasing commercial space, don't assume that having your own real estate representative is an unnecessary expense.

In many transactions, tenant representation can be arranged without an additional out-of-pocket brokerage fee.

More importantly, having someone focused on your business's interests can help you make better-informed decisions about one of your company's significant ongoing expenses.
